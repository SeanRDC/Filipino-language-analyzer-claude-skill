# Filipino ASR Analyzer & Dubbing Engine

## Summary
Enhances the agent's fluency in Tagalog by recovering speech from hard audio, correcting phonetic hallucinations from ASR, interpreting Taglish code-switching, and ensuring output cadence is natural enough for voice dubbing.

## When to Use
*   The user provides audio or video with Tagalog/Taglish speech, however noisy.
*   The user provides an ASR transcript containing mangled Tagalog or Taglish.
*   The user needs an English prompt translated into conversational, spoken Filipino for dubbing.
*   The user asks to clean, analyze, or respond to informal Filipino text.

## Register Policy
Lean on **modern conversational Tagalog** (Manila-style Taglish) by default. Deep Tagalog (*malalim na Tagalog*: literary, formal, poetic, or purist) must still be recognized and correctly interpreted when it appears in the input. Convert it to modern equivalents unless the user asks to keep the formal register. See [references/register.md](references/register.md).

*   **Transcripts are always verbatim.** Register conversion applies only to the interpretation and dubbing steps, never to the cleaned transcript.
*   **Keep performative formality.** When a speaker puts on a formal register for effect (a kid imitating a cooking show's *"Ngayon tayo ay magluluto na"*, a mock news anchor, a pageant answer, a teacher voice), the formality *is* the meaning or the joke. Keep it in dubbing, and note it in the analysis.

## Steps

### 0. Get the Raw Transcript and Gather Evidence (only when given audio/video)

**0a. Transcribe** with the bundled script (run from this skill's directory):

```bash
python scripts/transcribe.py <file-or-url> --out <transcript.txt>
```

*   **Device and load.** `--device auto` (the default) uses an NVIDIA GPU if there is one, then an Intel Arc GPU through OpenVINO, then the CPU. On the Intel GPU, a 3-minute clip takes about 6 s instead of 35 s, and the CPU and fans stay idle. OpenVINO decodes greedily (Intel's GPU has no beam search) in 30 s windows, because its long-file mode dropped the end of a clip. On the CPU, the script uses half the cores and low priority by default. Same transcript, just slower and cooler.
*   **Loop guard.** Greedy GPU decoding loops over music beds and shouting. With `small` it looped in 9 of 10 drama clips (*I'm sorry* ×14 over an action scene). Turbo looped in 1 of 10 full runs and 9 of 113 windows, all over music or screaming. When a 30 s window repeats one line or a block of lines (A B C A B C), or its times run backwards, the script keeps the lines before the loop and re-decodes the rest on the CPU with `small` and beam search. It lists that range under `# loop guard`. Lines in that range are the `small` model's evidence, not turbo's. Over music, `small` invents fluent English that nobody said, so drop those lines unless the `en` pass or a window also has them. Cues with Greek, Cyrillic or other non-Latin letters are listed as likely hallucinations; discard them.
*   **Two decoders beat two identical runs.** For step 0b.2, make run 1 the default (GPU, `large-v3-turbo`) run and run 2 `--device cpu --window 30` (`small`, beam search: a different model and decoder, so it fails in different places). Two faster-whisper CPU runs can come out word for word the same, so the second one adds nothing. A different decoder fails in different places. Run window and slow passes on the GPU: they are cheap there.
*   **Model.** The default `--model auto` picks `large-v3-turbo` on a GPU and `small` on CPU-only machines. In a blind test on 10 captioned Filipino drama clips, raw word accuracy on the held-out set was: `small` 54%, `medium` 66%, `large-v3-turbo` 75%. Turbo took about 10–25 s per clip on an Intel Arc iGPU and almost never looped. Use `--model small` on weak machines, and `large-v3` only when time doesn't matter.
*   Other defaults: language `tl`, VAD off, and Whisper's "this is silence" filter off. On noisy Tagalog speech, that filter threw away whole 30-second windows of real dialogue. Pass `--skip-quiet` only for long recordings with genuinely silent stretches.
*   Never use English-only `*.en` models. **Do not add an `initial_prompt`.** In testing, a Taglish prompt leaked its own words into the transcript or made Whisper drop whole lines.
*   Keep the raw output untouched and show it alongside your cleaned version, so the user can see every correction.
*   The script ends with a list of **gaps** (2 s+ with no cue, including a missing ending), then **hollow cues** (8 s or longer with very few words) and **wordless cues** (`...` or symbols only). Turbo hides lost speech in both, so they count as gaps. `--start`/`--end` (seconds or `mm:ss`) transcribe one window, `--window 30` decodes the whole range in overlapping 30 s windows (the zoom pass), and `--slow 0.75` stretches the audio first; timestamps stay in source time. See 0b, steps 1 and 3.
*   Requirements: `pip install -r scripts/requirements.txt`, plus `ffmpeg` on PATH.

**0b. Hard-audio routine.** When the audio is noisy, fast, overlapping or barely audible, or the transcript has gaps or gibberish, gather all of this before cleaning:

0. **Identify the language first.** Not every Filipino clip is Tagalog. Check hashtags (*#bisaya*, *#bisdak*, *#ilocano*), captions and marker words (Cebuano: *kaayo*, *karon*, *unsa*, *dili*, *naa*, *gyud*). Whisper has no option for most Philippine languages, so `tl` is the closest setting, but its output will be spelled like Tagalog. Transcribe in the **speaker's** language, never "Tagalogized", and watch for words that look the same but mean different things in the two languages (Cebuano *kaayo* "very" ≠ Tagalog *kayo* "you"). For Cebuano, see [references/bisaya-cebuano.md](references/bisaya-cebuano.md). For other languages, say so, and flag the transcript for a native speaker.
1. **Check every gap.** Go through the script's gap list (and any early ending) and test whether each stretch is really silent: `ffmpeg -i <media> -af silencedetect=n=-35dB:d=1 -f null -`. Sound with no transcript means missed speech, not silence. **Re-run each gap on its own** with `--start`/`--end`, padded by a second or two on each side (except at a narrator → film switch; see the window hygiene in step 3). A full-file pass often drops the first lines after a switch of speaker or language (narrator → film clip), and repeating the full run drops them again: all three full runs skipped four lines in a row that a 15-second window recovered.
    *   **Hidden gaps.** Treat every hollow or wordless cue the script lists as a gap, and re-run it as a window. Turbo doesn't leave silence where it lost speech. It stretches a short line over 10–25 s of music (one 10-word line covering 20 s, or 3 words over 11 s) or writes `...` cue after cue (×12 over a TV news insert, which dropped a whole narrated line). A hollow cue's start time is where the music starts, not the speech, so take its timing from the window.
2. **Run it 2–3 times.** Gibberish, or words from an unrelated language (e.g., German *darauf* in a Tagalog clip), means Whisper fell back to random sampling, so each run differs. Line the runs up and trust only the words that recur. Recurrence is not proof, though: two runs of the same decoder share their mistakes, so a translation leak can show up in both (*Let's go outside* ×6 in both `tl` runs, where windows heard *tara na, dun muna tayo sa labas*). **Count witnesses by model, not by file.** The turbo full run, its windows and its slow windows are one witness, and the `small` CPU run is a second. Three turbo files agreeing against `small` is one vote against one. Settle it by the syllables, the `en` gloss and the context. If it's still a tie, lean to turbo, which is far more accurate overall, except over music or inside a hollow cue: there it invents fluent English stock lines (*I don't know what to say*, *I love you*), and a reading the other witness shares beats them. On long recordings, the runs often match word for word on clean stretches and split apart only where the audio is really hard (overlapping shouting, sobbing). Spend your effort and your low-confidence tags there.
3. **Zoom in on hard stretches.** Re-run fast, shouted or overlapping stretches as short windows (5–30 s), once at normal speed and once with `--slow 0.75`. Line the window runs up with the full runs just as in step 2. Windows bring back dropped openers and particles (*Ke, isa…*, *Okay! Ikaw na!*), rapid-fire lists, and whole shouted sentences that a full run reduced to *Ay, tama na!* The slow run is best for names and particles. It loops and invents English lines more often than the normal-speed window, so don't trust a reading that only the slow run has. Re-run whenever:
    *   a cue covers far more time than its words could fill (a short line on a long cue means speech was lost);
    *   a line starts abruptly with no opener in heated or fast talk;
    *   the runs disagree, or speakers overlap.

    Window hygiene:
    *   **Start at the switch.** At a narrator → film switch, start the window at or just after the switch, not padded back into the narration. A window that opens on the narrator often returns the narrator's last words and then nothing for 20 s or more.
    *   **Loops mean a bad window.** If a window repeats one phrase five or more times (*I'm sorry* ×13, *Ayawad na* ×25), repeats a block of two or three lines, or its timestamps run backwards, discard it. Re-run it as a 5–10 s window, or with `--device cpu`. Greedy GPU decoding loops most over music beds and shouting. `--device cpu` (beam search) is the allowed fallback for these short windows. Lines before the loop starts are still usable evidence; only the looping part and everything after it are discarded.
    *   **Timing comes from the full runs.** Window timestamps can drift 2–4 s. Use them for words only. When the full runs drift apart (3–6 s on music-bed dramas), take the one whose cue boundaries match `silencedetect`.
    *   **Order and batching.** Finish all full runs first, then the gap and zoom windows. When the gap list is long (music-bed dramas list 15–40 gaps), merge gaps less than about 5 s apart into one window instead of re-running each. Then pass all the windows of a clip in **one call**: `--windows 0:50-1:15,6:15-6:40 --speeds 1,0.75`. Loading the model takes longer than decoding a window, so separate calls cost about 8 s each just for loading.
    *   **Hallucinations on quiet stretches.** The `en` pass can loop over real speech, and the CPU run can invent lines over a silent ending or music. A cue that only one run has, sitting over music or silence, is suspect.
4. **Make one English pass** (`--language en`). Whisper *translates* instead of transcribing, and that English gloss tells you what a garbled line is about (*"I'm just a friend"* → *kaibigan ko*; *"every month"* → *buwan-buwan*). Use it for meaning only; its wording is never what was said, and it can even flip the meaning (*muntik nang magpakamatay*, "almost killed himself", came out as *"why Mark died"*). When the sound and the gloss disagree, trust the sound. The English pass also silently skips long emotional Tagalog stretches, so a gap in it is not silence. On sparse or quiet audio the English pass can invent stock lines (*"Thank you so much for watching, see you in the next video"*, *"Please subscribe"*); discard them. **Exception:** for stretches spoken *in English* (move names, titles, English phrases), the English pass is a real transcription and beats both the `tl` spelling and your sense of what "should" be said (*window of eagle* → *wind of eagle*, not the more logical *wing*).
5. **Read the screen.** Pull frames (`ffmpeg -i <video> -vf fps=1 frames/%03d.jpg`, or crop and tile the caption band) and collect:
    *   **Broadcast subtitles** (TV news): close to ground truth. They expose translation leaks and acronym errors.
    *   **Fan captions** (TikTok/Reels/Shorts): evidence, not truth. They are split across frames and unpunctuated, so stitch them into sentences. Captioners mishear too (*"SA MUKHA ANG"* was *sa mukha kang*). When a caption breaks Tagalog grammar, trust the grammatical reading.
    *   **Post description, hashtags and the platform's search suggestion:** they often quote the key line (*"Burst fade parin to ya?"*, *"magluluto na original video"*, *"tatlong basong kutsara"*).
    *   **Livestream comments:** the speaker is answering them, which tells you who *kayo/n'yo* is and what was asked. Never transcribe comments as speech.
    *   **Scene:** setting, roles and props (police station, kitchen, interview with microphones) narrow the vocabulary.
    *   Captions in parentheses (*"(korean cough)"*, *"(laughs)"*) describe sounds; write them as `[sound: …]`, not as speech.
    *   Overlay titles added by re-uploaders (*"mga taong mahilig sa yosi"*) are meme framing, not dialogue.
    *   **Audio only?** Say so in the output. Without the screen you lose captions and who is speaking, so don't guess speakers from voice quality alone. Still split overlapping turns (step 7) and label them *A:* / *B:*, or with names the dialogue itself says (*Bash*, *Carla*, *Tita*).
6. **Identify the source.** Compilations, reaction videos and reuploads of famous films or teleseryes often name the title (narrator, file name, description). Remembered lines from a well-known scene are useful evidence for medium confidence, but they are never ground truth: memory of a quote is often slightly off, so the audio decides. A remembered line may only **choose between** readings that already fit the raw syllables. It must never replace them. Character names are the most useful thing a known source gives you, nicknames included (*Basha* is called *Bash*, which Whisper wrote as *Mas* / *Mat* / *Ma*). Once a name is established in the clip, a garbled word in the **address slot** (line start, line end, or before a comma or a plea) that sounds like it **is** that name: *but please, I need to know* = *Billy, please*; *Jay Valentine* = *Jade Valentin*. **First spelling of a name:** when no title, caption or description names a character and the runs disagree, decode the clearest instance (said alone or in the address slot, ideally in a slow window). Pick the real Filipino or English name whose consonants match most runs, then use that one spelling every time it appears in the clip, tagged medium. Never write different spellings for one character.
7. **Separate the speakers.** In fights and overlapping talk, Whisper merges two voices into one line, and the quieter voice is lost or glued on (*"Sinungaling ka pa!"* with *"Tama na!"* from the other actor underneath). Split a line into turns when:
    *   the voice changes (pitch, male/female) partway through;
    *   the second half answers or contradicts the first (*"…And you will never be Celine."* / *"Celine is dead. Hindi na siya babalik."*);
    *   the pronouns flip (*ako*↔*ikaw*);
    *   one speaker pleads (*Tama na! Awat na!*) under another's shouting.

    Label the turns *A:* / *B:* (or with names, once known). For each overlap, give both lines with their own confidence tags. When the output is a one-line-per-row table with no speaker column, keep overlapping turns in one cell, split with ` / ` (*A: Sinungaling ka pa! / B: Tama na!*), and give the row the lower of the two tags. Never drop the quieter turn because the format has no room for it. This is only for one cue that holds two voices. When the runs already split the turns into separate cues, keep them as separate rows, even if they start in the same second.

### 1. Reconstruct the Transcript (ASR Cleanup)
Work through the evidence in this order. The patterns behind each step, with verified examples, are in [references/asr-hallucinations.md](references/asr-hallucinations.md).

1.  **Anchor on the hard evidence.** Lines confirmed by subtitles, descriptions or captions are fixed points. Rebuild the rest around them.
2.  **Start from the raw syllables, not from the meaning.** The raw line is the default, and every change you make has to be justified by sound. If the raw already contains a real Tagalog word that fits the grammar, keep it, even when another word would make a smoother sentence. In session 2026-11-17-04, about half the reviewer's fixes were cases where the raw was right and the cleanup "improved" it away:
    *   *Ako ba* → *Ikaw ba*
    *   *pinasukan* → *pinatulan*
    *   *naging* → *magiging*
    *   *kating* → *bilis*
    *   *trabaho* → *pinagtrabahuhan*
    *   *Kayo … may gusto … di ako* → *ginusto ko na rin*

    Mishearings that are still close in sound, like *mga aga* (*mang-aagaw*), *dalamang* (*dalawang*) and *sumang utka* (*sumagot ka*), were swapped for unrelated words (*maganda*, *ilang*, *umuwi ka*). Two more ways the cleanup drifts from the raw:

    *   **Don't snap a name or taunt to the nearest dictionary word.** The full runs had *Alan Calbo*, and only a window run had *Aaleng*. The cleanup picked *Aleng kalbo* (the address term *aleng* plus its linker), but it was *Alen kalbo!* Take the linker *-ng* only when you can hear it.
    *   **A second burst is not a copy of the first.** When two garbled outbursts come back to back, decode each from its own syllables. *Tanginala* / *Tanggilalaw* was *Tangina naman! … Ampota!*, not the same curse twice.

    **Raw-first has limits.** A real word in the raw still loses when:
    *   **it is a stutter of the decoder.** A tripled syllable (*nambababay* → *nambabae*, not *nambababae*) or a doubled word is Whisper's glitch, not the speaker's.
    *   **the sentence's own logic needs another form.** *Akala n'yo … **na** sasaktan n'yo lang kami* is a hypothetical, so it is ***ma**sasaktan* (the *ma-/na-* swap). Aspect and mood must fit the frame (*akala*, *kung*, *sana*).
    *   **the video names the word.** The title, file name or the narrator's own intro beats a later mishearing of the same word: the intro says *confrontation scenes*, so the outro's *conversation scenes* is a mishearing.
    *   **all runs agree on a clean line.** Where the full runs match word for word on clear audio, keep that line. Slow and zoom windows are there to recover missing or garbled words. They don't get to re-decode a clean, agreeing line into a different but equally plausible one (*ang* ↔ *'yung*, *'yan* ↔ *'yon*, *tinatanaw* → *tinatahak*).
3.  **Decode by sound, word by word.** Sound each fragment out with Tagalog phonology in mind: *dy/ty/sy* are said as *j/ch/sh* (*siya na ja* = *sinadya*, *canko* = *tiyan ko*, *nanjan* = *nandiyan*). Then apply the sound patterns: vowel swaps (*kawale* → *kawali*), stop swaps (*napten* → *napkin*), v→b and f→p in English loanwords (*Bicap* → *V-cut*), collapsed reduplication (*nag-drog* → *nagdodroga*), words split at the wrong boundary (*naman tika* → *ng mantika*), address terms (*kaya* → *kuya*), and English nouns turned into verbs (*nagnanapten* → *nagnanapkin*). Pick the candidate with the fewest sound changes.
4.  **Catch Whisper's English-side errors.** In `tl` mode, Whisper bends shouted English and interjections into Tagalog-looking nonsense (*Sarate! Shoot!* ← *Attention!*). It also outputs English *translations* of Tagalog lines (*"What is CBDC?"* ← *"Ano 'yung CBD?"*), drifts acronyms (*CBD* → *CBDC*), and drops the small Tagalog words around English terms. Flag a bare English line inside a Tagalog exchange as a possible leak.
    *   **Leak or real English?** A leak is an English rendering of a line that windows or the `en` pass hear differently, usually in Tagalog. A Taglish speaker's real English (*It's up to you!*, *I deserve an explanation*, *Stop acting like you own it*) keeps its English shape across every run, even when garbled (*And you had it up to you!*). Keep it. Dropping a real English line is as wrong as keeping a leak.
    *   English speech works the other way: the cleaned line must keep the Tagalog words an English speaker uses. *"their past, who gots"* is *their past hugots*, not "past hurts". Never swap a Filipino loanword for its English meaning.
    *   **English lines inside Tagalog dialogue.** Taglish characters switch to whole English sentences (*Dad will take care of everything.*, *We'll raise this kid together!*). The `tl` runs bend them into Tagalog-looking junk (*babe well*, *baby*), so for every line that sounds English in any run, take the `en` pass's words for it.
    *   Whisper also trims fixed English idioms (*"Between the lines"* ← *Read between the lines*), so restore the whole idiom when its core is there.
5.  **Match the domain vocabulary.** Guess the domain from the scene (food, haircuts, drugs, school, games, quarrels), then test garbled nouns against its words. Whisper turns Filipino dish names (*English plan* ← *leche flan*) and English jargon (*Mersfade* ← *burst fade*) into nonsense.
    *   **Quarrels and cheating** have their own idioms. A word that sounds odd but matches one of them is probably right (*sumawsaw*, "to dip in", is to cheat; *kating-kati*, "itching", is to be eager for someone new; *pinasukan*; *mang-aagaw*). Read the scene's story to know which set to test against, then let the sound pick within it. See [references/slang-glossary.md](references/slang-glossary.md).
6.  **Use the conversation's structure.**
    *   **The speaker's own repeated phrases:** a clear *"Paano mo nasabing mabilisan?"* later decodes *"Paano nga sabi?"* earlier.
    *   **The interview turn pattern:** question → dodge → the question repeated → answer.
    *   **Rhetorical frames.** Emotional monologues repeat one frame (*May kulang ba sa 'kin? May mali ba sa 'kin?*, *Sana maisip mo rin…* ×4, *Ako na 'yung tama, ako na 'yung mali…*). Decode the clearest instance, then use it as the template for the garbled ones. The template proposes words; it doesn't insert them. Add a template word only when some run or window has a sound for it. The template carries over **only within one speaker's run**. Don't carry it across scenes. Session 2026-11-17-04 forced a line from another scene onto two different lines that were really *O, bakit ngayon ayaw mong aminin?* and *O baka naman kaya ayaw mong aminin…*.
    *   **Parallel halves and echoes.** A line often mirrors itself or the line before it. *Kung ayaw mo sa 'kin, ayaw ko rin sa'yo*; *nandito na ako… nandiyan kayo*; *magmukhang tanga* followed by *na magmukha kang tanga*; a lone *Ikaw na!* opening a list of *ako na 'yung…*. When half a pair is clear, rebuild the other half to match it.
    *   **The reply decodes the line before it.** Read the next turn before you settle a garbled line. A teacher answering *"O, sige. Mura pa."* ("Go on, curse some more") means the student's *Hindi ako muntang* was *Hindi ako 'yon, tangina…*, not *Hindi ako, ma'am!* A rally leader's *Lalaban tayo!* followed by a crowd *Oo!* (the `en` pass had *"Are we fighting? Yes!"*) means a reply turn was dropped. When the next line reacts to something (a curse, a yes, a laugh, an insult) that the cleaned line doesn't contain, the cleaned line is wrong or a turn is missing.
    *   **Procedural order** in tutorials and recipes: *"Pag mainit na ang kawali…"* means the step before was heating (*pagpapainit … ng mantika*).
7.  **Check the grammar fits.** The verb decides the marker (*lagyan … ng*, *ilagay … ang*), and linkers attach where they belong (*nasabing*, *siyang*). A mismatch means a word was misheard.
    *   **Aspect comes from the syllables and the timeline.** Keep the aspect Whisper heard (*bumuo*, *naging*). Check it against when the event happened: a finished event takes the completed form (*ikaw rin ang **bumuo** kung ano ang **naging** siya*). Ongoing thought takes the repeated syllable even when Whisper collapsed it (*inisip* → ***iniisip*** when it means "you think"). **Which wins:** keep the heard aspect unless a known sound pattern explains the change (a collapsed or doubled syllable, a *ma-/na-* swap). Then the timeline decides. Never change aspect on the story alone.
    *   **Modals take the infinitive.** After *kailangan*, *dapat*, *puwede* and *kaya* ("can"), the verb is in its infinitive form (*kailangan nating **mag-usap***, *dapat **kumain** ka*), not the future or ongoing form. A doubled syllable there (*mag-uusap*) is the decoder's doubled syllable, so remove it.
    *   **No reason, no change.** When the raw form is a real word and neither a sound pattern nor a grammar marker (a modal, *akala*, *kung*, *sana*, a time word like *kahapon* or *bukas*) forces another form, keep the raw form, even when another one reads better. Tag the line medium if the other form is close.
    *   **Pronouns follow who is talking to whom.** Recheck *ko/mo/niya*, *sa'yo/sa kanya* and *kayo/siya* against the addressee. *"Ilang beses akong lumapit **sa'yo** para balikan **siya**"* is said to the sister about the boyfriend.
8.  **Preserve what was heard.**
    *   Never delete a small word Whisper actually caught (*na*, *nga*, *pa*, *eh*, *po*, *lang*), and keep English discourse markers (*so*, *okay*, *actually*).
    *   **Keep Taglish's doubled markers.** Taglish speakers put a Tagalog marker after an English preposition: *about **sa** online class*, *regarding **sa** project*. It looks redundant in English, but it is what was said. Both `tl` runs had *about sa online class*, and the cleanup dropped *sa* because the `en` pass read *about online class*. The `en` pass's wording must never leak into the cleaned line.
    *   **Listen for the openers and particles Whisper drops in fast or heated speech.** These include line openers (*O*, *O ano*, *Eh*, *Ah*, *Ke*, *Hmm* as sarcastic agreement) and mid-line particles (*nga*, *na*, *lang*, *pa rin*, *eh*, *mas*). They change the tone (*Eh ikaw!* is a retort) or the meaning (*mas importante*, *dahil **lang***). A line that starts abruptly in an argument probably lost its opener, so re-run that window (0b, step 3).
    *   **An odd first word is often a fused opener, not noise.** Vlog and story openers (*So ayon*, *Ayun*, *Ayan*, *Tapos*) and agreement replies (*Oo nga*, *Oo nga, eh*, *'no?*, *Kaya nga*) get glued into one fake word: *Sayonan dito* = *So ayon, dito*; *Gwang hain no* = *Oo nga, eh, 'no*. Before you drop or replace a strange first word, try splitting it into one of these.
    *   **Don't add what wasn't said, either.** Inserting *na lang* (*binalik **na lang** kita*) is as wrong as deleting it. The test for any word you add: at least one run or window has a sound for it. Exception: a function word the grammar cannot do without (*Alam **mo** bang…*, *in **Starting Over Again***) may be added, at lower confidence.
    *   **Keep the speaker's short forms.** Write *'di*, *ba ko*, *'to*, *n'ya*, *'yung* when they are said that way, and expand nothing (*Pangit ba ko?*, not *Pangit ba ako?*). Keep intensifier reduplication (*lahat-lahat*, *sakit-sakit*, *kating-kati*). Keep Spanish-derived Filipino forms as spoken (*responsibilidad*, *sakripisyo*), and don't anglicize them (*responsibility*).
    *   **Short forms cut both ways.** Don't expand a contraction, and don't invent one either. *Ayoko/ayokong* (from *ayaw ko*) is one word when said that way, so don't split it into *ayaw ko*. ***Ang* and *'yung* are different words.** Write the one that was said, and don't turn *ang* into *'yung* to sound casual (*ikaw **ang** namilit*, *mabayaran **ang** inutang mo*). The same goes for *hindi* vs. *'di* and *ako* vs. *'ko*.
    *   Reattach split affixes with hyphens (*nag-drive*, *i-send*).
    *   Restore stress and glottal stops from context (*bása*/*basâ*, *búkas*/*bukás*).
    *   Cleanup repairs what was misheard; it does not tidy up the speaker's grammar.
9.  **Never abandon a stretch.** Write the full best-guess sentence with a confidence tag (high / medium / low). A specific guess helps the reviewer more than "[unclear]". Mark edit cuts at clip edges as cut (*"…to sa ating…"*) instead of inventing words. Add a non-speech row (`[music, no speech]`) only for stretches of 5 s or more without speech. Shorter pauses between lines need no row. Non-speech rows get tags too. Use high only when `silencedetect` shows silence or every run and window is empty there, and medium over a music bed where every run is empty. Use low when any run had a stray line, a hollow cue or a wordless cue there, because that stretch may still hide speech.
10. **Audit each line against the raw before you hand it in.** Line up cleaned and raw, word by word. For every word you changed, name the sound pattern that justifies it. If you can't, or if the raw word was already valid Tagalog that fits, go back to the raw. Lower the confidence tag on any line that still differs a lot from every run.

### 2. Semantic Analysis
*   Extract the core intent of the cleaned transcript, including who says what and why it is funny or notable.
*   Map informal slang, euphemisms or regionalisms (e.g., *charot*, *lodi*, *omsim*, *amats*, *dinadatnan*) to their semantic equivalents before formulating a response.

### 3. Localization & Dubbing Output (If generating spoken response)
*   **Ask which language to dub into** when the source isn't Tagalog (e.g., keep Bisaya or convert to Tagalog). Don't assume.
*   **Match Cadence:** Literal translations sound overly formal and robotic in Tagalog. Translate the *intent*, not the literal words.
*   **Use Conversational Taglish:** Where natural to a native speaker in an everyday setting, keep standard English loanwords rather than forcing purist coinages (e.g., use "airplane" or *eroplano* instead of *salipawpaw*).
*   **Syllable Pacing:** If the output is for dubbing, keep sentence length and syllable count as close to the source audio timing as possible. Use contractions (e.g., *'yung* instead of *iyong*, *sa'yo* instead of *sa iyo*) to speed up the sentence.

## Gotchas
*   Never output rigid, textbook Filipino (formal or academic register) unless explicitly requested. Manila-style conversational Taglish is the default for natural dubbing.
*   Do not "correct" grammatically accepted Taglish (like *nag-a-update*) into pure English or pure Tagalog.
*   **Never "correct" the speaker's own mistake.** A phrase that makes no sense (*"tatlong basong kutsara"*, "three glasses of spoon") may be exactly what was said, and is often the joke the meme exists for. If the post description, captions or search suggestion quote the odd phrase, it was really said, so keep it verbatim and explain it in the analysis. The same goes for invented names in parodies (*wind of eagle*, *isnik dragon*): absurd is on purpose, so don't "improve" them into what makes sense. Fix only what the ASR misheard.
*   **Sound beats story.** When ASR output is ambiguous, pick the reconstruction closest in sound, not the one that best fits the scene or makes the strongest punchline. Theme-driven guessing turned *Bulihin* into "shoot" (*babarilin*) when it was really "catch" (*huhulihin*), and the English gloss *"add oil"* turned *papay* into *ilalagay* when it was *pagpapainit*. The story should help you find the right *set* of words (quarrel idioms, kitchen verbs). Within that set, the sound decides. Writing a cleaner, more "sensible" line than the audio supports is the most common error in long emotional clips.
*   **Never clean up profanity, and never add politeness.** In memes the curse is usually the punchline, so transcribe it verbatim. Whisper hides curses in odd fragments (*muntang* = *'yon tangina*, *Tanginala* = *Tangina naman*, *Pag-tangin* = *Putangina*). Don't fill a garbled spot with what a polite speaker "should" say: a student talking to a teacher got *ma'am* when they had said *tangina*. Test unexplained fragments in a heated or comic line against the curse list in [references/slang-glossary.md](references/slang-glossary.md). The reverse also holds: a curse needs syllables. If a `tl` run has a near-curse (*ina mo*, *tangi na*), decode it as the curse. If only the `en` gloss curses (*Fuck!*) and no `tl` run has curse-like sounds, the gloss is a lead, not proof. Keep the sound reading and tag it low.
*   Watch out for false friends: Spanish loanwords in Tagalog often have slightly different meanings than in modern Spanish.
